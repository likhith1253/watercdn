Water utilities choose pump speeds and pressure-reducing-valve settings after a leak with evolutionary optimisers that call a hydraulic simulator thousands of times. Leaks recur, so every solved case leaves a finished run behind, and a new search can start from the best solutions of an old one. Sometimes this warm start saves most of the budget; sometimes it traps the search. We treat the choice as a decision: before spending any budget, warm-start from one named past run or start fresh, maximising the expected gain while keeping the rate of harmful transfers below a level alpha set by the operator. A gradient-boosted predictor estimates the matched-budget utility of each candidate source, and a Learn-then-Test gate turns its harm score into an accept/abstain rule with a finite-sample bound. To measure utility rather than guess it, we built 2,640 paired source-target cases on two networks (Net3 and the 782-junction L-Town), with fresh and warm runs sharing random numbers. The benefit of a named warm start is predictable before the run (Spearman 0.58; 0.67 with a probe), while the similarity rule used by most transfer methods is not (0.05). Where harm is rare, the gate keeps its bound in all of 200 re-splits and captures 87.5% of the oracle gain; where harm is common (16% of pairs on L-Town), the bound holds but costs most of the benefit with 70 calibration cases. After three pipe closures the realised harm of a frozen gate reaches 23% against a nominal 20%; a shift-aware, density-ratio-weighted gate detects this and abstains. Monitoring warm runs and aborting them gave no gain, and the transfer balance changes when differential evolution is replaced by a genetic algorithm. Code and the paired-run benchmark are released.
